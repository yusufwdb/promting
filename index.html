01 · Introduction to Prompt Engineering

Big Idea: A language model produces a continuation of the information it receives. Prompt engineering is the practice of making that continuation useful, relevant, and reliable.

🖥 Interactive Presentation Slides · Module 1
Slide 1 of 4 · The Core Dilemma
The Central Question of Prompt Engineering

What makes a model's next words useful for a specific engineering task?

  • Open-Ended Continuation: Given “The most important discovery was…”, thousands of continuations are valid. The surrounding text acts as a steering wheel.
  • Not Just Clever Phrasing: In real applications, prompt engineering means deciding what data to retrieve, how to structure multi-turn memory, and how to validate outputs.
Slide 2 of 4 · The Timeline
From N-Grams to Autoregressive Transformers
  • 1940s+: Statistical Language Models: Markov models predicting next words based on 1–3 preceding words. Zero long-range understanding.
  • 2014: Seq2Seq Models: Encoder-decoder architecture compressing entire sentences into a single fixed "thought vector".
  • 2015: Attention in Neural Sequence Models: The decoder queries all source token representations instead of one bottleneck vector.
  • 2017: The Transformer: Attention Is All You Need replaces recurrence entirely with stacked self-attention.
Slide 3 of 4 · The GPT Formula
Autoregressive Mathematical Generation

GPT-style generation repeatedly estimates conditional probability distributions:

$$P(t_{n+1} \mid t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_n)$$
  • G (Generative): Synthesizes new text token-by-token.
  • P (Pre-trained): Learns broad language patterns from web-scale data before task specialization.
  • T (Transformer): Processes all token relationships simultaneously using multi-head attention.
Slide 4 of 4 · The 3 Inescapable Limitations
Crucial Boundaries to Remember
  • 1. Not a Search Engine: Models calculate statistical continuations; they do not natively verify truth or query sources without tools.
  • 2. Context Windows are Finite: Information must be snippetized, ranked, and prioritized.
  • 3. Fluency ≠ Correctness: Confident, grammatical prose can still contain subtle mathematical, logical, or factual errors.

1. The Bottleneck: A Sentence Is Not a Single Vector

Early 2014 sequence-to-sequence translation models consisted of two parts: an encoder that read the source text and compressed it into a single fixed-dimension vector (often called a thought vector), and a decoder that unpacked that vector to emit the target translation.

While this worked for short phrases like “How are you?”, it completely collapsed when translating complex legal, technical, or literary paragraphs. Preserving names, numerical qualifiers, dependent clauses, and stylistic nuances inside a single fixed-size mathematical vector created an extreme information bottleneck. Important context was lost before decoding finished.

❌ Without Attention (Classical Seq2Seq)

One fixed summary vector must carry all information. Early context vanishes in long sequences. Unavoidable memory bottleneck.

✓ With Attention (Modern Architecture)

The decoder dynamically cross-references all source word representations at every generation step. Relevant context is directly accessible.

02 · Understanding How Language Models Work

Big Idea: To use a language model well, understand what it actually processes: tokens, probability distributions, and context. Many prompting mistakes become easy to diagnose once these mechanics are clear.

🖥 Interactive Presentation Slides · Module 2
Slide 1 of 3 · Atomic Representation
Tokens: How Language Is Digitized

Humans see letters and words; language models receive token integer IDs:

  • Subword Splitting: Tokenizers like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) assign single IDs to common words, but split rare words (un-believ-able) into chunks.
  • Character-Level Failures: Letter counting, palindromes, and anagrams fail because characters are blended inside token IDs.
  • Token Inflation: Non-English languages (Turkish, Polish, Arabic) consume 2–3× more tokens per sentence than English.
Slide 2 of 3 · Probability Mathematics
Logits, Softmax & Temperature Scaling

At every step, the transformer computes raw scores (logits $z_i$). Softmax normalizes them:

$$p_i(T) = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)}$$
  • Temperature = 0.0: Pure greedy decoding. The single highest logit is selected with 100% determinism.
  • Temperature > 1.0: Flattens differences, increasing sampling probability of less likely words.
  • Key Insight: Low temperature guarantees predictability, NOT accuracy!
Slide 3 of 3 · Self-Attention & Latency
Attention Q/K/V and Prefill vs Generation
  • Query (Q): What information am I seeking?
  • Key (K): What information do I contain?
  • Value (V): What representation do I pass forward?
  • Prefill Phase (Parallel): Prompt tokens processed all at once on GPU matrix cores.
  • Generation Phase (Sequential): Output tokens generated one by one. The primary latency bottleneck!

1. The Generation Loop in Action

AUTOREGRESSIVE GENERATION PSEUDOCODE
def autoregressive_generate(prompt_text, max_tokens, temperature):
    token_ids = tokenizer.encode(prompt_text)
    
    for _ in range(max_tokens):
        # 1. Forward pass through transformer layers
        logits = model.forward(token_ids) # shape: [vocab_size]
        
        # 2. Rescale logits by temperature
        scaled_logits = logits / max(temperature, 1e-5)
        
        # 3. Softmax into normalized probability distribution
        probabilities = softmax(scaled_logits)
        
        # 4. Sample next token ID
        next_token_id = sample(probabilities)
        
        # 5. Append to context sequence
        token_ids.append(next_token_id)
        if next_token_id == tokenizer.eos_token_id:
            break
            
    return tokenizer.decode(token_ids)

2. Hallucinations Taxonomy & The 5 Defenses

A hallucination occurs when an LLM emits unsupported, factually false, or fabricated information presented with persuasive confidence.

Factual Hallucinations

Fabricating historical dates, inventing clinical dosages, or attributing quotes to the wrong historical figures.

Logical Contradictions

Reaching mathematical conclusions or deductive claims that directly contradict numbers supplied in the prompt.

Structural Hallucinations

Inventing fictitious book titles, realistic-looking DOIs, nonexistent GitHub repositories, or invalid API methods.

The 5 Production Engineering Defenses:
1. Source Grounding: Always provide verified excerpts in the prompt context.
2. Explicit Negative Fallback: Instruct the model: “If the provided documentation does not state the answer, reply that it is unknown.”
3. Tool Grounding: Offload volatile, private, or real-time information to external APIs.
4. External Code/Schema Checks: Run generated code in a sandbox; validate JSON against Pydantic schemas.
5. Task Decomposition: Break large reasoning tasks into staged subtasks with intermediate validation gates.

03 · Chat Models, Human Feedback, and Tools

Big Idea: A base model completes text. A chat assistant is aligned to participate in structured conversations, respect system boundaries, and operate tools safely.

🖥 Interactive Presentation Slides · Module 3
Slide 1 of 3 · The Alignment Pipeline
From Pre-training to RLHF
  • Pre-training: Massive web crawl (~500B+ tokens). Model learns statistical grammar and broad associations.
  • SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning): Human experts write thousands of ideal question/answer pairs to teach helpful conversational structure.
  • Reward Modeling: Humans rank competing model outputs. A reward neural network learns human preference scoring.
  • RLHF with PPO: The model is optimized using reinforcement learning to maximize reward scores while minimizing sycophancy.
Slide 2 of 3 · ChatML & Security
Structured Dialogue Roles
  • system: High-priority instructions defining persona and negative rules.
  • user: Human input; must be treated as untrusted data.
  • assistant: Model responses and proposed tool execution calls.
  • tool: Output from external APIs and databases.
Slide 3 of 3 · Function Calling
The Tool Calling Execution Loop
  • The LLM never runs code directly. It emits structured JSON arguments matching a defined schema.
  • The surrounding software application executes the actual HTTP/DB call.
  • The software feeds the result back to the model as a tool role message.
  • The model interprets the response and explains it clearly to the human user.

The Little Red Riding Hood Principle

Models generate text by matching statistical patterns. When a prompt uses a widely recognized, canonical format (such as standard dialogue, technical troubleshooting reports, or formal JSON schemas), the expected continuation is much easier for the model to infer accurately.

Application Evaluation Strategies

Offline Testing (Pre-Deployment)

Evaluate against benchmark datasets of 50–500 test cases with known ground truth. Check JSON schema validity rates and execute generated code in isolated sandboxes.

Online Telemetry (Production Monitoring)

Measure real-world user acceptance rates of suggestions, downstream edit distances (how much the user had to rewrite), task completion, and session duration.

05 · Prompt Content, Context, and RAG

Big Idea: The quality of an LLM response depends fundamentally on which information enters the prompt. The core engineering challenge is to find, prioritize, and present the most useful context.

🖥 Interactive Presentation Slides · Module 5
Slide 1 of 2 · Retrieval Mathematics
Lexical vs Neural Semantic Matching
  • Lexical (Jaccard / BM25): $J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}$. Compares exact character sets and keywords. Essential for error codes, part numbers, and IDs.
  • Neural (Cosine Embeddings): $\operatorname{cosim}(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\| \|\mathbf{b}\|}$. Compares angles of high-dimensional vectors. Excels at synonyms, paraphrasing, and multilingual queries.
Slide 2 of 2 · Architecture
The Two Independent RAG Pipelines
  • Pipeline A: Offline Indexing: Collect documents → Split into sliding-window chunks → Generate embeddings with embedding model → Store vectors and metadata in vector DB. (The generative LLM is never called here!).
  • Pipeline B: Online Request-Time: Embed user query → Nearest-neighbor cosine search in vector index → Assemble prompt with top-$k$ snippets → Generative LLM creates grounded answer.

Sliding-Window Chunking with Overlap

In production RAG systems, breaking documents into chunks is necessary to prevent ideas split across sentence boundaries from being severed. A sliding window uses a defined window size and stride:

PRODUCTION PYTHON SLIDING WINDOW CHUNKER
def snippetize(text: str, window_words: int = 200, stride_words: int = 120) -> list[str]:
    """
    Splits text into chunks of 200 words with 80 words overlap (stride=120).
    Ensures contextual continuity across chunk boundaries.
    """
    words = text.split()
    chunks = []
    for start in range(0, len(words), stride_words):
        chunk = words[start:start + window_words]
        if not chunk:
            break
        chunks.append(" ".join(chunk))
        if start + window_words >= len(words):
            break
    return chunks

Chekhov’s Gun in Prompt Engineering

If an irrelevant detail, tangential paragraph, or noisy snippet is injected into the context window, the model will often assume it must be essential and force it into the output. Improving retrieval quality is vastly more valuable than simply increasing the number of retrieved chunks.

06 · Prompt Engineering Laboratory Workbook

Your Mission: Stop treating prompts as magic phrases. Treat them as reproducible experimental designs: make a hypothesis, test alternatives, record evidence, and document what changed.

Lab 1: Interactive Fallacy Case Explorer (8 Cases)

Inspect the arguments used in the 24 experimental runs. Click each card to reveal the expert ground-truth analysis:

Case A

“Sales started falling after we introduced a new logo. This proves that the logo change discouraged customers.”

Intended Category: False Cause (Post hoc ergo propter hoc)
Core Issue: Temporal sequence alone does not establish causation. Other factors (market trends, seasonality, competitor actions) were ignored without justification.
Case B

“This supplement must work because a famous actor said in an interview that it made them feel great.”

Intended Category: Appeal to Authority (Invalid Authority)
Core Issue: A celebrity endorsement does not constitute clinical, peer-reviewed, or pharmacological evidence.
Case C

“I met two rude waiters at this restaurant chain. Clearly, the entire company has terrible staff.”

Intended Category: Hasty Generalization
Core Issue: A sample size of two isolated individuals cannot generalize across hundreds of employees in an entire company.
Case D

“If we allow students to use phones in class, within a year nobody will pay attention and the education system will collapse.”

Intended Category: Slippery Slope
Core Issue: Asserts an extreme catastrophic chain of events without establishing that each subsequent link is an inevitable consequence.
Case E

“If we do not vote for this bill, our economy will collapse catastrophically and we will all lose our savings.”

Intended Category: Appeal to Fear (Argumentum ad metum)
Core Issue: Uses extreme emotional panic and fear to force agreement rather than presenting empirical macroeconomic evidence.
Case F

“We should not listen to his tax-reform proposal because he changed jobs twice within one year.”

Intended Category: Ad Hominem
Core Issue: Attacks a person's employment history rather than analyzing the fiscal merits or flaws of the tax reform proposal.
Case G

“Either you support a complete ban on internal-combustion cars, or you do not care about the environment at all.”

Intended Category: False Dilemma (Bifurcation)
Core Issue: Artificially collapses a complex spectrum of environmental policies into two extreme binary positions.
Case H

“A study involving 10,000 people indicates that regular physical activity reduces the risk of heart disease. Therefore, it is worth introducing programs that encourage older adults to exercise.”

Intended Category: No Clear Fallacy (Sound Practical Argument)
Core Issue: Cites substantial empirical data in support of a proportionate, qualified policy recommendation.
